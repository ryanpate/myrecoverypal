# What's working in recovery short-form video (researched 2026-11-24)

This decays fast. Re-run the research before the next 30-day batch.

## How sure this is

Each finding is marked:

- **Observed**: I opened the post on Instagram on 2026-11-24 and read its numbers (see the next section).
- **Reported**: a named press or industry source said it.
- **Inferred**: a pattern across several sources, or my judgement.

YouTube was not scanned. TikTok was scanned the same day (section after Instagram). Instagram doesn't show view counts on the web, so the numbers below are likes, comments, reposts and shares. Shares are the one to watch: they are what carries a Reel to non-followers.

**What this shows**

1. **One explainer can dwarf everything else.** The "flat stretch" video got 26× the likes of the graphic it was built on. The topic (why early sobriety feels boring, and that it passes) plus a simple visual is the strongest proven recovery idea in this sample. Comments are full of day counts ("Day 65, at the boring stage").
2. **Comment-a-keyword CTAs are everywhere** among the bigger recovery accounts (CLEAR, COMMUNITY, FRIDAY). They inflate comments, which helps reach, and hand the account a DM. They need someone, or an auto-DM tool, to send the link.
3. **The top recovery posts are faces.** dradi, marathon2sobriety and midlife.mentor are all on camera. Faceless posts that did badly were reposted celebrity clips and an AI-generated account (10 likes). A faceless account has to win with the idea and the visual.
4. **Dry January virality is comedy, and much of it is pro-drinking.** The biggest #dryjanuary posts are jokes with shares far above likes. A calm how-to won't go viral there. One dry, self-aware joke per week can ride the tag; the how-tos serve the people who joined.
5. **DUI "what to do" Reels get more shares than likes.** Lawyers' practical videos (26K+ shares) show the court audience shares useful, specific steps. We can't give legal advice, but "the three non-legal things to do this week" fits the same shape.
6. **Court-ordered recovery content is small but it starts arguments.** "You can't court order someone into recovery" drew 375 comments on 240 likes. Few people make practical court-attendance content at all; nobody in this sample did.

**What TikTok adds to the Instagram picture**

1. **The withdrawal/body timeline is TikTok's strongest topic for us,** and it can be done without a face: the 41.3K video is a timeline written on screen over a trending sound. The top results all lead with "see a doctor", so the safety line doesn't cost reach.
2. **Dry January on TikTok isn't only jokes.** Comedy and brand ads top it, but practical tip lists (70.3K) and before/after results (91.6K) are close behind, unlike Instagram. Day 15's "three things before Dry January" has a real audience here.
3. **Boredom is smaller on TikTok** (top result 6K) than on Instagram (213K). Keep Day 26 as an Instagram-first video.
4. **Loneliness is a recurring hook** ("nobody to hang out with", 1.6K shares; Instagram's midlife list says the same). Not yet in the scripts; it's the backup topic for a "repeat the winner" slot.
5. **Holiday-sober content is small on TikTok** (top first-Christmas result 784 likes). Don't expect the December holiday scripts to travel far; "Pt 1" series framing is what the top one used.
6. **Court-ordered TikTok is jokes and complaints.** The one that spread (7K) is a relatable joke about chasing a signature. Practical court content is again missing. DUI lawyers do far less on TikTok than on Instagram.
7. **Transformation ("0 days → 500 days") is the biggest recovery format,** and it needs a real person's before and after. Not something this account can do in first person.

## Hooks that recur

Patterns, with a recovery version of each. Quoted lines are mine, written to the pattern.

1. **Name the exact moment.** "The first Friday night sober." Specific beats general in every hook guide reviewed.
2. **POV.** "POV: you're the only one not drinking at the office party."
3. **Timeline promise.** "What your body does in the first 72 hours without alcohol."
4. **Stop doing / myth.** "Stop saying 'I can't drink.' Say this instead."
5. **The thing nobody tells you.** "Nobody warns you that your sleep can get worse before it gets better."
6. **Direct address to one viewer.** "If a judge told you to go to meetings and prove it, this is for you."
7. **Number on screen.** "$4,380. That's a $12 bottle of wine a day for a year."
8. **Show the object.** A printed sheet, a phone screen, a medallion, in frame in the first second. No words needed for the first beat.

## Formats

- **Text on screen plus voiceover over hands, paper or a phone screen.** Fits the faceless rule (`court-reels-later.md`) and the advice that the first frame must explain the video with the sound off.
- **Screen recording of a tool doing the thing** (calculator, Craving SOS, Anchor, the tracker). Proof in under five seconds.
- **Numbered list, one item per cut** (3 to 5 items). Easy to save and share.
- **POV scene** with on-screen captions over b-roll (a hallway, a kitchen, a parking lot at night).
- **Quiet slow text** (one line per beat over a still shot). Lower views, high saves, suits sensitive topics.
- **Series** ("Week one, day 3"). Gives people a reason to follow. Dry January suits it.

## Platform notes

The same vertical clip can go to TikTok, Instagram Reels, Facebook Reels and YouTube Shorts. Hook guides agree on: no intro, payoff stated within 3 seconds, on-screen text in the first frame, and check each platform's retention graph for the drop in the first 3–5 seconds. Specific "decision window" numbers (1.5 s, 1.7 s, 3 s) vary by source and have no traceable study behind them; treat them as rough.

## The gap the account can own

Calm, practical, faceless answers to specific problems: proof of attendance, getting through a named night, what to do in the ten minutes a craving lasts. Most recovery content is personal storytelling, which this account can't do in first person. Utility it can do, and every utility video has a free tool behind it.
